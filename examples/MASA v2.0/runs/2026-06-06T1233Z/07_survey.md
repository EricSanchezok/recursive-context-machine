# Automated Literature Survey Generation Using Large Language Models: Agent Architectures, Retrieval-Augmented Pipelines, and Evaluation Methodologies

## 1. Introduction and Scope

### Defining the landscape of LLM-based automated survey generation

The volume of scientific publications has grown exponentially, with over 4 million papers published annually across all disciplines. For researchers, the burden of staying current with the literature in their field has become untenable — a challenge that the traditional practice of writing narrative literature surveys aims to address but cannot scale. In response, a rapidly growing body of work has emerged at the intersection of large language models (LLMs) and automated survey generation: systems that take a user-provided topic or query and produce a structured, citation-grounded literature survey by orchestrating retrieval, planning, drafting, and refinement sub-tasks. This paper surveys that body of work.

**Scope.** We review LLM-based systems that produce structured literature surveys from paper corpora, with a focus on work published between 2023 and 2025. Our analysis is organized along three primary axes: (1) **agent architecture** — how systems decompose the survey-writing task into roles, agents, and interaction protocols; (2) **retrieval-augmented pipeline design** — how evidence is collected, filtered, and synthesized; and (3) **evaluation methodology** — how survey quality is measured through benchmarks, metrics, and human protocols. We explicitly exclude general-purpose RAG systems not evaluated on survey or article-writing tasks, single-paper summarization tools unless embedded in a survey pipeline, traditional pre-LLM systematic review tools, and non-English survey generation.

**Existing surveys.** Several prior reviews have mapped adjacent territory. A systematic review of 34 studies covering the 2008–2023 period cataloged AI tools for systematic review automation [arXiv:2401.10917]. A meta-review of 172 studies on LLM usage in literature review creation charted the early adoption landscape [arXiv:2409.04600]. A survey of 21 SLR tools and 11 LLM-based tools compared capabilities across the research lifecycle [arXiv:2402.08565]. More broadly, surveys on AI-driven research support [arXiv:2503.01424] and LLM-assisted scientific discovery [arXiv:2502.05151] have contextualized automated survey generation within the larger scientific AI ecosystem. However, no existing survey provides a dedicated, architecturally grounded treatment of LLM-based survey generation systems themselves — their agent designs, retrieval pipelines, citation mechanisms, and evaluation frameworks — which is the gap this paper addresses.

**Contributions.** This paper makes the following contributions: (1) a structured taxonomy of architectural patterns — single-agent, multi-agent, and hybrid — with representative systems analyzed for each; (2) a comparative analysis of planning and outline generation strategies; (3) a systematic treatment of retrieval-augmented pipelines, from query formulation through multi-source synthesis; (4) a dedicated examination of citation attribution and factuality mechanisms, including sentence-level citation, citation graph expansion, and post-hoc factuality verification; (5) a comprehensive review of evaluation benchmarks, datasets, metrics, and human evaluation protocols; (6) a forward-looking discussion of emerging frontiers, including interactive generation, living surveys, and advanced multi-agent coordination; and (7) a roadmap of open challenges spanning hallucination, evaluation standardization, knowledge freshness, domain adaptation, multi-modal content, and user steering.

**Reader roadmap.** This paper is organized around four anchor questions derived from the survey specification. **Primary anchor question — architectural patterns:** What are the dominant architectural patterns for LLM-based survey generation, and how do they compare in terms of agent composition, retrieval integration, and output quality? This question is addressed in Section 2. **Secondary anchor question 1 — evaluation quality:** How do existing systems evaluate survey quality, and what are the limitations of current benchmarks, metrics, and human evaluation protocols? This question is addressed in Section 6. **Secondary anchor question 2 — citation graph expansion:** What role does citation graph expansion play in improving survey coverage and citation quality? This question is addressed in Section 5.3 within the broader treatment of citation attribution and factuality mechanisms in Section 5. **Secondary anchor question 3 — key limitations:** What are the key limitations of current automated survey generation systems, and what directions should future research pursue? This question is addressed in Section 8. The remaining sections provide supporting analyses: Section 3 examines planning and outline generation, Section 4 covers retrieval-augmented pipelines, Section 7 explores emerging frontiers, and Section 9 concludes with practical recommendations.

## 2. Architectural Taxonomy of Automated Survey Generation

### Single-agent, multi-agent, and hybrid architectures for LLM-based survey writing

Automated survey generation systems can be classified by their underlying agent architecture — how they decompose the survey-writing task into computational units, assign roles, sequence operations, and manage information flow. This section presents a taxonomy of three broad architectural patterns: single-agent systems that centralize all sub-tasks in a single LLM invocation or tool-augmented agent; multi-agent systems that distribute sub-tasks across specialized agents with defined coordination protocols; and hybrid and interactive systems that combine automation with human-in-the-loop capabilities. We survey representative systems within each category and conclude with a comparative analysis that maps architectural choices to use-case suitability.

### 2.1 Single-Agent Architectures

Single-agent architectures treat survey generation as a pipeline of sub-tasks orchestrated by a single LLM augmented with retrieval tools, memory mechanisms, and structured prompts. The foundational system in this category is **AutoSurvey** [arXiv:2406.10252], which pioneered a four-stage workflow — retrieval, outline generation, subsection drafting, and refinement — coordinated through a single LLM backbone. AutoSurvey demonstrated that a well-prompted single agent could produce coherent multi-section surveys, establishing the baseline architecture for the field.

Several extensions have since refined this paradigm. **SurveyX** [arXiv:2502.14776] introduces a two-phase Preparation+Generation pipeline with an **AttributeTree** data structure that pre-processes source papers into structured attribute representations (e.g., problem definition, method, results), enabling more systematic content organization during drafting. The **LitLLM toolkit** [arXiv:2402.01788] provides a modular RAG-based toolkit with explicit keyword extraction, query construction, and a re-ranking pipeline, offering a configurable single-agent workflow for scientific literature review. **SurveyGen-I** [arXiv:2508.14317] adds adaptive planning through a coarse-to-fine retrieval mechanism: it first retrieves broadly to understand the topic landscape, then narrows retrieval for each subsection as the outline evolves, guided by a memory module that tracks already-covered content. **SurveyForge** [arXiv:2503.04629] contributes outline heuristics learned from human-written survey structures, coupled with a scholar navigation agent for targeted paper retrieval, and demonstrates that single-agent systems can achieve competitive evaluation scores when equipped with domain-specific structural priors.

A common thread across single-agent architectures is the **retrieval → outline → draft → refine** workflow. The single LLM handles all reasoning steps, from formulating search queries to revising drafted text. This simplicity reduces coordination overhead and makes single-agent systems easier to deploy and debug, but it also creates a bottleneck: the same LLM context window must simultaneously maintain global coherence across sections while attending to fine-grained citation details. As a result, single-agent systems tend to perform best on narrowly scoped topics where the paper corpus is well-defined and inter-section dependencies are limited [arXiv:2406.10252; arXiv:2503.04629].

### 2.2 Multi-Agent Architectures

Multi-agent architectures distribute the survey-writing task across multiple specialized LLM agents, each responsible for a distinct sub-task such as retrieval, planning, drafting, reviewing, or citation verification. This subsection surveys coordination patterns (sequential, parallel, roundtable), representative systems, and observed performance benefits.

The highest-performing system in current benchmarks is **Agentic AutoSurvey** [arXiv:2509.18661], which achieves an 8.18/10 overall score through a four-agent framework: a Manager agent oversees the workflow, a Searcher agent handles retrieval, a Writer agent produces draft text, and a Reviewer agent critiques and refines the output. The agents operate in a sequential pipeline with feedback loops, enabling iterative quality improvement without overloading any single agent's context. **SurveyG** [arXiv:2510.07733] introduces a 3-layer hierarchical citation graph (Foundation/Development/Frontier) and deploys three specialized agents to expand, verify, and organize citations at each layer, demonstrating that multi-agent designs can explicitly optimize for citation coverage breadth. **LiRA** [arXiv:2510.05138] employs a multi-agent collaborative workflow where agents engage in structured discussions about section content, claim accuracy, and citation relevance before committing text, achieving reliability and readability improvements over single-agent baselines. **ResearchPilot** [arXiv:2603.14629] takes a local-first approach, using DSPy, SQLite, and Qdrant for retrieval-augmented multi-agent literature synthesis without relying on proprietary APIs, addressing deployability concerns in the multi-agent paradigm.

Beyond these full systems, several mechanism-focused papers investigate coordination patterns for multi-agent survey generation. **MATC** [arXiv:2508.04306] studies three collaboration paradigms — sequential, parallel, and roundtable — finding that roundtable discussions among agents improve error mitigation in long-form literature reviews but at higher token cost. [Note: The sequential/parallel/roundtable taxonomy in MATC operates at the coordination-protocol level; this is distinct from the exploration/exploitation/experience labels used in other multi-agent frameworks, which describe strategy-level agent behavior rather than interaction structure.] **Mixture of Knowledge Minigraph Agents** [arXiv:2411.06159] proposes composing small, knowledge-specialized agents that each contribute a "minigraph" of related work, which are then merged into the final survey section. **Select, Read, and Write** [arXiv:2505.19647] decomposes the task into three roles — a Selector agent identifies relevant papers, a Reader agent extracts key claims with graph-aware reading, and a Writer agent synthesizes the selected content — demonstrating that even a simple three-agent decomposition improves citation precision over monolithic generation.

The primary advantage of multi-agent architectures is **specialization**: each agent maintains a focused context window and responsibility, reducing cognitive load and cross-contamination between retrieval, reasoning, and writing sub-tasks. The cost is increased system complexity, token consumption from inter-agent communication, and coordination overhead in managing agent handoffs [arXiv:2509.18661; arXiv:2508.04306].

### 2.3 Hybrid and Interactive Architectures

A third architectural pattern combines automated generation with human oversight, either through modular replaceable components or direct user intervention in the generation process. These systems recognize that fully automated surveys may miss domain-specific nuances, and that expert guidance at key decision points — outline structure, citation selection, or claim framing — can substantially improve output quality.

**LLM×MapReduce-V3** [arXiv:2510.10890] employs an MCP (Model Context Protocol) architecture with modular servers for retrieval, drafting, and verification, connected through a message-passing bus that supports human-in-the-loop intervention at each stage. **InteractiveSurvey** [arXiv:2504.08762] enables users to customize intermediate components — the outline, the paper pool, and section-level drafting parameters — through an iterative interaction loop where the system presents intermediate outputs for user feedback before proceeding. **AutoSurvey2** [arXiv:2510.26012] extends the original AutoSurvey with parallel section generation (drafting multiple sections concurrently) and real-time retrieval that allows users to inject new papers mid-generation, achieving a 2× speedup over its predecessor. **IterSurvey** [arXiv:2510.21900] introduces recurrent outline generation, where the system generates an initial outline, gathers user feedback, revises the outline, and then proceeds to drafting, with the option for multiple refinement cycles.

The seminal work on hybrid generation for structured knowledge synthesis is **STORM** [arXiv:2402.14207], which, while originally designed for Wikipedia-like article generation, established the pattern of leveraging an LLM to simulate multi-perspective questioning and searching before drafting. STORM's approach — generate questions from different angles, retrieve answers, then synthesize into a coherent article — has directly influenced several survey generation systems [arXiv:2504.08762; arXiv:2510.26012].

Hybrid architectures occupy a middle ground between full automation and manual writing, and they are particularly attractive for expert-authored survey projects where quality requirements are high and the cost of error is large. The key design challenge is determining the optimal points and frequency of human intervention: too little defeats the purpose of automation, while too much negates its efficiency benefits [arXiv:2504.08762; arXiv:2510.10890].

### 2.4 Comparative Analysis

| Dimension | Single-Agent | Multi-Agent | Hybrid/Interactive |
|-----------|-------------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Number of LLM agents** | 1 | 3–4 (typical) | 1 + human |
| **Retrieval strategy** | Tool-augmented single agent | Dedicated searcher/retriever agent | User-guided retrieval |
| **Planning mechanism** | Prompted outline via single LLM | Specialized planner agent or discussion | User-revised outline |
| **Evaluation scores (SurveyBench)** | 5–7 / 10 | 7–8.18 / 10 | 6–7.5 / 10 |
| **Human-in-the-loop** | None | None by default | Required or optional |
| **System complexity** | Low | High | Medium |
| **Token cost per survey** | Low–Medium | High | Medium–High |
| **Best use case** | Narrow domain, rapid prototyping | Broad coverage, high-quality requirements | Expert-guided surveys, high-stakes domains |

Single-agent architectures are best suited for rapid prototyping, constrained domains where the paper corpus is manageable, and scenarios where development resources are limited. Multi-agent architectures excel at broad-coverage surveys requiring high citation quality and organizational coherence, as demonstrated by Agentic AutoSurvey's leading benchmark score [arXiv:2509.18661]. Hybrid architectures are optimal for expert-guided survey projects, educational settings where transparency is valued, and high-stakes domains where verification by humans is mandatory. As the field matures, we observe convergence: multi-agent systems are beginning to incorporate human-in-the-loop checkpoints [arXiv:2510.10890], while hybrid systems are adding automated agent sub-routines for repetitive sub-tasks [arXiv:2510.26012]. Notably, STORM [arXiv:2402.14207] (discussed in Section 2.3) established the foundational pattern of multi-perspective questioning and searching before drafting that has influenced the design of both multi-agent and hybrid systems, serving as a bridge between the two architectural paradigms.

## 3. Planning and Outline Generation Strategies

### From hierarchical decomposition to iterative refinement — how survey systems structure content

The planning stage — determining the structure, scope, and sequence of a survey before drafting — is arguably the most consequential design decision in automated survey generation. A poorly structured outline propagates errors through all downstream stages, while a well-designed plan constrains retrieval and drafting to produce coherent, well-organized surveys. This section reviews how current systems approach planning, from one-shot hierarchical decomposition to adaptive and iterative refinement strategies.

### 3.1 Hierarchical Outline Decomposition

The simplest and most common planning strategy is hierarchical outline decomposition, where the system generates a multi-level outline (sections, subsections, and sometimes bullet points) before any drafting begins. AutoSurvey's original design employed a fixed outline template with section-level headings inferred from the topic, then populated each subsection through independent retrieval-and-drafting cycles [arXiv:2406.10252]. **SurveyForge** improved on this by learning outline heuristics from a corpus of human-written survey outlines, enabling the system to predict section structures that align with disciplinary conventions — for example, distinguishing the "Method → Results → Discussion" flow typical of empirical surveys from the "Concept → Taxonomy → Open Problems" flow of conceptual surveys [arXiv:2503.04629].

**SurveyX**'s AttributeTree [arXiv:2502.14776] represents a more structured approach to hierarchical decomposition. Rather than generating a flat outline, it constructs a tree of attributes (problem definition, methodology, experimental setup, findings, limitations) for each source paper, then organizes survey sections around these attribute clusters. This ensures that every section in the final survey covers comparable dimensions across cited papers, improving structural consistency. **IterSurvey** [arXiv:2510.21900] employs a Section-Outline → Subsection-Detail pipeline where the initial section outline is generated, each subsection is expanded into a detailed plan with key points and target citations, and the detailed plans are validated for coverage overlap before drafting begins.

The broader literature on structured content generation for surveys includes **Generating Related Work** [arXiv:2104.08668], which demonstrated that explicitly planning the rhetorical structure of a related-work section before writing improves coherence and citation placement — a direct precursor to the outline-first approaches adopted by survey generation systems. **SurveySum** [arXiv:2408.16444] addresses the sub-task of multi-document summarization into survey sections, providing a dataset and framework for section-level content planning from collections of scientific papers. These works provide theoretical and empirical grounding for the hierarchical decomposition strategies used in current survey systems.

### 3.2 Adaptive and Evolving Planning

A limitation of one-shot hierarchical decomposition is that the initial outline may miss emerging themes, fail to allocate appropriate depth to sub-topics, or become outdated as retrieval reveals unexpected paper distributions. Adaptive planning strategies address this by treating the outline as an evolving artifact that is revised as the system accumulates evidence.

**SurveyGen-I** [arXiv:2508.14317] implements evolving plans through a memory-guided writing mechanism: as each subsection is drafted, key claims and coverage gaps are logged in an external memory module, and the plan for the next subsection is adjusted accordingly. This creates a closed loop where planning and drafting inform each other. **CogWriter** [arXiv:2502.12568] brings a cognitive writing perspective to long-form generation, introducing a hierarchical planner that monitors the completeness of each planned section against the available evidence and dynamically adjusts section scope. **SuperWriter** [arXiv:2506.04180] applies hierarchical DPO (Direct Preference Optimization) and Monte Carlo Tree Search to the planning problem, treating outline generation as a sequential decision process where the system explores alternative section structures and selects the one that maximizes expected coverage and coherence.

The key insight across adaptive approaches is that planning is not a one-shot template-filling operation but a **dynamic process** that evolves in response to evidence accumulation and coverage assessment. This distinction is crucial for surveys covering rapidly evolving fields where the novelty or importance of sub-topics may shift during the generation process itself [arXiv:2508.14317; arXiv:2506.04180].

### 3.3 Iterative Refinement and Self-Feedback

Beyond adaptive planning that adjusts to evidence, some systems incorporate explicit **refinement loops** that critique and revise the outline or draft based on self-generated feedback. The foundational work in this area is **Self-Refine** [arXiv:2303.17651], which demonstrated that a single LLM could generate output, provide feedback on its own output, and refine it — all within a single model invocation pattern. This paradigm has been extended to survey generation through **EIPE-text** [arXiv:2310.08185], which adds evaluation-guided iterative plan extraction: after drafting, the system evaluates the plan against quality criteria (coverage, coherence, specificity) and extracts an improved plan, which then guides re-drafting. **SuperWriter**'s structured thinking-through process [arXiv:2506.04180] extends this to multiple refinement stages — outline refinement, draft refinement, and citation refinement — each guided by self-critique and preference optimization.

Refinement loops are particularly valuable for survey generation because the planning and drafting stages have different optimization criteria. A plan optimized for coverage may yield a draft that lacks coherence, and vice versa. Iterative refinement allows the system to explore the trade-off space between these competing objectives [arXiv:2303.17651; arXiv:2310.08185]. This refinement-at-the-plan-level (Section 3.3) operates at a different granularity from the Generate-then-Refine method for citation improvement discussed in Section 5.2, but both approaches share the same underlying principle of separating generation from critique to improve output quality without over-constraining the generative process.

### 3.4 Planning Quality and Coverage

The quality of the planning stage directly predicts overall survey quality, making it one of the most important design levers in system development. SurveyBench introduces a **coverage breadth** metric that measures how comprehensively a survey's outline covers the topical sub-areas of a given query (see Section 6.1 for a full description of SurveyBench's design and evaluation dimensions). This metric is computed by comparing the set of topics mentioned in the outline against a reference topic taxonomy derived from the query's retrieval results, providing a quantitative measure of planning completeness.

Analysis using SurveyBench reveals a strong correlation between outline coverage breadth and human-rated survey quality — the study reports correlation coefficients above 0.7 across multiple evaluation dimensions. This correlation was established through a protocol in which human annotators rated surveys on five dimensions (coverage, coherence, accuracy, readability, usefulness) while blind to the system that produced them, and these ratings were then correlated with the automated coverage breadth metric. The finding has important implications for system design: improvements to planning strategies — whether through better hierarchical decomposition, adaptive plan evolution, or iterative self-feedback — are likely to yield proportionate improvements in final output quality.

However, planning quality itself remains challenging to evaluate. Existing approaches rely on human annotation of outline completeness, comparison to gold-standard outlines, or automated coverage analysis against taxonomic topic models. Standardized planning evaluation — including shared protocols for measuring outline coverage, depth, and logical flow — would benefit the field by enabling direct comparison of planning strategies across systems. SurveyBench's coverage breadth metric (Section 6.1) is a step in this direction, but its reliance on a specific topic taxonomy construction method means that results may not generalize across domains. We revisit the evaluation standardization challenge in Section 8.2.

## 4. Retrieval-Augmented Pipelines for Evidence Collection

### Query formulation, evidence extraction, and multi-source synthesis for survey content

Survey generation systems depend critically on their ability to retrieve, select, and synthesize evidence from scientific papers. Unlike general-purpose RAG systems that answer specific questions, survey generation requires retrieving broadly across a topic area, extracting claims from multiple papers, and synthesizing them into a coherent narrative with proper attribution. This section reviews the four stages of retrieval-augmented pipelines in survey generation: query formulation, evidence extraction and re-ranking, adaptive retrieval with self-reflection, and multi-source synthesis.

### 4.1 Query Formulation Strategies

The first stage of any retrieval pipeline is converting a topic or section heading into effective search queries. The **LitLLM toolkit** [arXiv:2402.01788] extracts keywords from the topic description and constructs multiple queries with different granularities (broad field queries, specific technique queries, and author-centric queries), then merges results to maximize coverage. **SurveyForge** [arXiv:2503.04629] introduces a scholar navigation agent that uses the outline structure to generate targeted queries for each section, informed by a citation graph of influential papers in the domain. **SurveyX** [arXiv:2502.14776] leverages its AttributeTree representation to formulate attribute-aware queries: for a section on "Methods," it queries for papers with relevant method attributes; for "Results," it queries for experimental findings. **SurveyGen-I** [arXiv:2508.14317] employs a coarse-to-fine retrieval strategy: initial queries are broad (e.g., "deep learning for NLP survey"), and as the outline evolves, queries become progressively narrower (e.g., "transformer architecture for text classification 2023"). This progressive narrowing helps systems avoid missing important sub-topics that broad initial queries might overlook.

A common challenge across query formulation strategies is balancing **recall** (ensuring all relevant papers are retrieved) with **precision** (avoiding irrelevant or low-quality papers that clutter the evidence pool). Systems typically address this through multi-stage querying — starting broad, then filtering based on established relevance criteria — rather than attempting a single optimal query [arXiv:2402.01788; arXiv:2508.14317].

### 4.2 Evidence Extraction and Re-ranking

Once papers are retrieved, the system must extract the most informative spans of text and rank them by relevance to the target section. The **LitLLM toolkit** [arXiv:2402.01788] implements a re-ranking pipeline that scores extracted passages using cross-encoder relevance models, selecting the top-K passages for each section. **LitFM** [arXiv:2409.12177] introduces the first literature foundation model with a graph-based retriever that encodes citation graph structure into the retrieval process, achieving a 28.1% precision improvement over text-only retrieval by leveraging citation relationships to identify papers that are both content-relevant and citation-connected. **PUREsuggest** [arXiv:2408.02508] offers an interactive citation suggestion system that uses keyword steering to iteratively refine the paper pool based on user guidance.

Evidence granularity is an important design dimension. Some systems extract full abstracts or individual sentences, while others extract structured information (e.g., problem-method-result triples). Finer granularity improves citation precision but increases the number of extraction decisions, potentially introducing noise. Coarser granularity is simpler but may include irrelevant content that dilutes survey focus [arXiv:2409.12177; arXiv:2402.01788]. Notably, LitFM's graph-based approach and LitLLM's cross-encoder approach represent complementary strategies for evidence re-ranking: LitFM leverages citation graph structure to identify papers that are citationally connected to the seed set, while LitLLM relies on dense passage representations without graph awareness, prioritizing fine-grained semantic relevance over structural context.

### 4.3 Adaptive Retrieval and Self-Reflection

A significant advance in retrieval-augmented generation is the paradigm of **adaptive retrieval**, where the LLM decides whether and when to retrieve external information based on its confidence, the nature of the query, and the completeness of already-retrieved evidence. The foundational work in this area is **Self-RAG** [arXiv:2310.11511], which introduces reflection tokens that enable the model to assess its own retrieval needs — deciding whether to retrieve, whether retrieved passages are relevant, and whether they support the generated claim. While Self-RAG was demonstrated on QA tasks, its adaptive retrieval + reflection paradigm has direct applicability to survey generation, where sections vary in their reliance on external evidence.

Several extensions of the Self-RAG paradigm are relevant to survey generation. **Self-Routing RAG** [arXiv:2504.01018] enables dynamic decisions between external retrieval and the model's parametric knowledge, reducing retrieval calls by 29% without sacrificing factual accuracy — particularly relevant for survey sections where the LLM may already have sufficient knowledge (e.g., well-known concepts) and does not need to retrieve. **FoRAG** [arXiv:2406.13779] optimizes for factuality in retrieval-augmented generation through doubly fine-grained RLHF, rewarding both retrieval correctness and generation factuality. **InstructRAG** [arXiv:2406.13629] introduces self-synthesized rationales for denoising retrieved passages, teaching the model to distinguish supporting from non-supporting evidence. **RA-RAG** [arXiv:2410.22954] adds source reliability estimation, cross-checking claims across multiple sources and weighting more reliable sources higher — a capability that is particularly valuable for survey generation where the authority of cited papers matters.

For survey generation, adaptive retrieval offers a natural fit: survey sections covering established knowledge may require minimal retrieval, while sections covering cutting-edge developments benefit from exhaustive search. The ability to dynamically allocate retrieval effort across sections could substantially reduce the token cost of survey generation while maintaining or improving coverage quality [arXiv:2310.11511; arXiv:2504.01018]. We note that these adaptive retrieval methods have been validated primarily on QA and summarization tasks; their transfer to the multi-section survey generation setting, while promising, remains an empirical question.

### 4.4 Multi-Source Synthesis and Citation Grounding

The final retrieval stage is synthesizing evidence from multiple papers into coherent narrative text with proper citation grounding. **OpenScholar** [arXiv:2411.14199] is a retrieval-augmented model specifically designed for scientific literature synthesis, incorporating a large-scale datastore of 45 million open-access papers and a structured retrieval pipeline that identifies the most relevant passages for each section. **DimInd** [arXiv:2504.18496] introduces facet-based synthesis, where the system identifies distinct analytical facets of a topic (e.g., theoretical foundations, empirical findings, open problems) and synthesizes evidence for each facet separately before assembling them into a coherent survey. This facet decomposition helps ensure that multiple perspectives are represented within each section. These two systems illustrate contrasting design philosophies: OpenScholar prioritizes breadth of coverage via scalable retrieval from a massive datastore, while DimInd emphasizes structured organization via human-guided faceting, enabling users to define the analytical lenses through which the literature is synthesized.

The foundational RAG paper [arXiv:2005.11401] established the retrieve-then-generate paradigm that all survey systems build upon, though survey generation introduces specific requirements not present in general RAG: multi-document synthesis (synthesizing claims from dozens of papers rather than a few), claim attribution (linking each claim to its source), and structural coherence (ensuring the synthesis follows a logical outline rather than a simple concatenation of retrieved passages). These requirements demand integration between the retrieval pipeline and the planning and attribution mechanisms described in Sections 3 and 5 respectively.

## 5. Citation Attribution and Factuality Mechanisms

### Ensuring groundedness through sentence-level citations, verification, and factuality evaluation

A defining requirement of automated survey generation is that every factual claim must be attributable to a specific source paper. Unlike general text generation where fluency is the primary quality criterion, survey generation demands **citation fidelity** — the generated text must correctly represent the content of the cited paper, and every claim should ideally be supported by at least one citation. This section reviews mechanisms for sentence-level and sub-sentence citation, examines factors affecting citation capacity and quality, discusses citation graph expansion for coverage, and surveys factuality evaluation methods for long-form text.

### 5.1 Sentence-Level and Sub-Sentence Citation

Early RAG-based survey systems typically cited at the paragraph or section level, providing a list of references without specifying which sentences they supported. Recent work has developed increasingly fine-grained attribution mechanisms. **ReClaim** [arXiv:2407.01796] introduces interleaved reference-claim generation: the model generates a sentence, immediately generates the reference that supports it, and then proceeds to the next sentence, achieving 90% citation accuracy on scientific QA tasks. This interleaved approach prevents the common failure mode of generating claims first and then retroactively assigning citations, which often yields mismatched or fabricated references. **VeriCite** [arXiv:2510.11394] implements a three-stage citation verification pipeline — candidate generation, NLI-based evidence selection, and citation assignment — that operates as a post-generation verification layer, correcting citation errors in drafts produced by any underlying system. **MIRAGE** [arXiv:2406.13663] takes a different approach, using saliency-based attribution methods from the model's internal representations to attribute generated claims to specific input passages without requiring explicit citation generation, enabling plug-and-play attribution for black-box RAG systems.

The most fine-grained approach is **Sub-Sentence Citations** [arXiv:2509.20859], which assigns citations to individual phrases within a sentence using a credit model that distributes attribution weight across multiple sources based on phrase-level content overlap. This addresses the issue that a single sentence often synthesizes information from multiple papers. **ARC-JSD** [arXiv:2505.16415] proposes a Jensen-Shannon Divergence-driven attribution method that requires no fine-tuning, measuring the distributional similarity between generated text and candidate source passages to determine attribution, which can be applied post-hoc to any LLM-based survey system.

The trend toward finer citation granularity — paragraph → sentence → sub-sentence — reflects the recognition that coarse citation undermines verifiability, a core requirement for scholarly surveys. However, the trade-off is increased computational cost and the risk of over-attribution, where trivial or well-known statements are burdened with citations that add no scholarly value [arXiv:2407.01796; arXiv:2509.20859].

### 5.2 Citation Capacity and Quality

Not all LLMs are equally capable of generating accurate citations, and the factors affecting citation quality are only beginning to be understood. A systematic analysis in *Citation Generation Capacity* [arXiv:2410.11217] evaluates LLMs across multiple dimensions: citation precision (what fraction of citations correctly support the claim), citation recall (what fraction of claims are cited), and citation hallucination rate (what fraction of citations are fabricated). The study finds that citation quality degrades with longer generation contexts, suggesting a **Generate-then-Refine** method — generating text without citations first, then retroactively inserting citations — that the authors report improves citation precision by 8–12% compared to generating with citations simultaneously. Source document access also matters: providing full-text access to cited papers improves citation precision by 15% over providing only abstracts, and model scale correlates positively with citation quality, with larger models (70B+ parameters) generating significantly fewer hallucinated citations than smaller models per the study's findings [arXiv:2410.11217]. These results have direct implications for survey system design, suggesting that systems should prioritize full-text access and post-generation citation refinement.

### 5.3 Citation Graph Expansion for Coverage

Beyond ensuring that individual claims are correctly cited, survey systems must also ensure **coverage breadth** — that the cited papers represent the full scope of relevant work rather than a narrow or biased subset. Citation graph expansion techniques address this by using the citation relationships among seed papers to identify additional relevant work. **SurveyG** [arXiv:2510.07733] constructs a 3-layer hierarchical citation graph distinguishing Foundation papers (seminal work), Development papers (incremental advances), and Frontier papers (most recent developments), and generates separate survey sections for each layer, ensuring that the final survey covers both historical context and cutting-edge work. **LitFM** [arXiv:2409.12177] integrates a graph-based retriever that traverses citation networks to identify papers that are citationally distant but topically relevant — a capability that text-only retrieval may miss. **PUREsuggest** [arXiv:2408.02508] supports interactive exploration of citation graphs, allowing users to steer the paper pool toward particular sub-topics or time periods through interactive citation suggestion. **CitationIE** [arXiv:2106.01560] provides foundational methodology for leveraging citation graphs in information extraction, though its focus is on structured extraction rather than survey generation.

Citation graph expansion addresses a fundamental limitation of keyword-based retrieval: relevant papers may not share vocabulary with the query, particularly for newer concepts that use different terminology than the established literature. Graph-based expansion complements text-based retrieval by identifying papers through citation relationships, which often indicate topical relevance even in the absence of lexical overlap [arXiv:2510.07733; arXiv:2409.12177].

### 5.4 Factuality Evaluation in Long-Form Text

Factuality evaluation for survey text is a distinct challenge from factuality evaluation for short-form QA, because surveys contain dozens of claims spanning multiple papers, and each claim must be verified against its attributed source. The methods for evaluating factuality in long-form text can be grouped by approach type.

**Decomposition-based methods** break generated text into atomic facts and verify each one independently. **FActScore** [arXiv:2305.14251] provides the most widely adopted approach of this class: it decomposes text into minimal verifiable units (atomic facts), verifies each fact against a knowledge source, and reports factual precision as the fraction of supported atomic facts. For survey text, this means splitting each sentence into 3–5 atomic facts (e.g., method name, claimed performance, comparison statement, year of publication) and verifying each one independently.

**Search-augmented methods** extend verification beyond the model's parametric knowledge. **SAFE** (Search-Augmented Factuality Evaluation) [arXiv:2403.18802] uses a search engine to verify factual claims against web sources, making it applicable to survey text where the LLM's parametric knowledge may be incomplete. **VERISCORE** [arXiv:2406.19276] evaluates factuality by extracting verifiable claims from generated text, checking them against a source document corpus, and computing recall (what fraction of source claims are correctly reported) and precision (what fraction of generated claims are supported by sources).

**Entity-grounded methods** focus on specific error types common in survey generation. **WildHallucinations** [arXiv:2407.17468] provides an evaluation benchmark specifically designed for long-form generation, tagging hallucinations at the entity level (fabricated paper titles, incorrect author names, non-existent publication venues).

**Fine-tuning-based approaches** address the problem from a training perspective. **FINETUNE-RAG** [arXiv:2505.10792] shows that fine-tuning LLMs to resist hallucination under imperfect retrieval conditions reduces hallucination rates by 40–60% on scientific text, suggesting that factuality improvements at the model level can complement post-hoc verification methods.

For survey generation, the most critical factuality dimension is **citation fidelity** — whether the generated survey accurately represents the content of the cited papers. This goes beyond checking whether citations are real to checking whether the attributed claims match the cited papers' actual content — a substantially harder evaluation problem that current benchmarks address only partially [arXiv:2406.19276; arXiv:2403.18802].

## 6. Evaluation Methodologies and Benchmarks

### Metrics, datasets, and human evaluation protocols for assessing survey quality

As the field of automated survey generation has grown, so too has the ecosystem of evaluation frameworks designed to measure system quality. This section reviews dedicated survey-generation benchmarks, large-scale datasets, individual evaluation metrics and protocols, hallucination evaluation methods, and human evaluation protocols, providing a comprehensive map of how survey quality is currently assessed.

### 6.1 Dedicated Survey-Generation Benchmarks

Six dedicated benchmarks have been released for automated survey generation, each with different design choices regarding topic coverage, evaluation dimensions, and metric granularity.

**SurveyBench** [arXiv:2510.03120] is the largest and most comprehensive benchmark, covering 11,343 topics with 4,947 generated surveys evaluated through a multifaceted metric hierarchy. Its distinctive feature is quiz-driven evaluation: for each survey, it generates reading comprehension questions and measures how well the survey enables answering them, providing a functional assessment of survey quality. The metric hierarchy spans coverage breadth, citation accuracy, factual consistency, and logical coherence. **SurGE** [arXiv:2508.15658] focuses on the computer science domain, providing a 1M+ paper retrieval pool and a 4-dimension evaluation framework (coverage, coherence, accuracy, readability). **SurveyEval** [arXiv:2512.02763] uses a 3-dimension evaluation across 7 subjects, combining LLM-as-a-Judge ratings with human reference surveys. **DeepSurvey-Bench** [arXiv:2601.15307] introduces academic value dimensions — informational value, scholarly value, and research guidance value — that assess not just textual quality but the survey's utility for downstream research. **SGSimEval** [arXiv:2508.11310] combines LLM scoring with quantitative metrics and human preference ratings, demonstrating that composite scores are more reliable than any single signal. **SurveyLens** [arXiv:2602.11238] provides 1,000 human-written surveys across 10 disciplines and introduces a dual-lens evaluation — automatic metrics complemented by discipline-specific human rubrics.

|-----------|------|--------|------------|----------------|------------|
| SurveyBench | 2025 | 11,343 | Coverage, Citation, Factuality, Coherence | N/A (topics only) | Yes |
| SurGE | 2025 | CS-domain | Coverage, Coherence, Accuracy, Readability | 1M+ papers | Yes |
| SurveyEval | 2025 | 7 subjects | 3 dimensions | N/A | Yes + LLM |

A critical observation is that no two benchmarks evaluate exactly the same set of dimensions or use the same scoring methodology. This makes cross-benchmark comparison of survey generation systems difficult, a challenge we discuss in Section 8.2.

### 6.2 Datasets for Survey Generation

Large-scale datasets serve both as training resources and as standardized evaluation sets for survey generation. **SciReviewGen** [arXiv:2305.15186] is the largest dataset of human-written scientific reviews, containing 10,000+ reviews with 690,000 cited papers across multiple disciplines. Its analysis revealed that hallucination is the primary challenge in automated survey generation, with human-written reviews containing near-zero hallucination rates but LLM-generated surveys exhibiting rates of 15–25%. **HierCat** [arXiv:2304.03512] provides 7,600 hierarchical catalogues with 389,000 reference papers, focusing on the structured outline level rather than full survey text. **Gen-Review** [arXiv:2510.21192] contains 81,000 LLM-generated peer reviews (ICLR 2018–2025), enabling study of AI-generated review detection but limited in applicability to survey generation due to the different genre. **SurveyGen** [arXiv:2508.17647] provides 4,200+ human-written surveys with the QUAL-SG framework for structured human evaluation, offering both training data and protocol templates for evaluation. **SurveySum** [arXiv:2408.16444] focuses on the sub-task of multi-document summarization into survey sections, providing a dataset of section-level summaries from scientific articles. Together, these datasets span different granularities — from full surveys (SciReviewGen, SurveyGen) to outlines (HierCat) to sections (SurveySum) — but their varying formats, domains, and annotation schemes make it difficult to use them as a combined evaluation suite.

### 6.3 Evaluation Metrics and Protocols

Beyond dedicated benchmarks, several evaluation protocols and metrics have been proposed for assessing survey quality. **PROXYQA** [arXiv:2401.15042] introduces the concept of meta-questions with proxy-questions and pre-annotated answers, enabling automated evaluation that correlates with human judgement through a self-consistency mechanism. The **LitLLMs evaluation study** [arXiv:2412.15249] — distinct from the **LitLLM toolkit** discussed in Sections 2.1, 4.1, and 4.2, which is a RAG-based pipeline system — proposes a zero-shot evaluation protocol using a rolling test set to avoid data contamination. The evaluation study finds that benchmark topics may overlap with LLM training data, inflating reported performance, and its protocol generates surveys on topics published after the LLM's training cutoff, ensuring that evaluation measures genuine generation capability rather than memorization. **Auto-survey Challenge** [arXiv:2310.04480] framed survey generation as a competition task with simulated peer-review evaluation, providing an early prototype of multi-system comparison. **Outcome-based Evaluation** [arXiv:2306.17614] takes a different approach, measuring not the textual quality of generated surveys but the impact of including or excluding specific studies on the survey's conclusions — a paradigm that aligns with systematic review methodology but has not been widely adopted by LLM-based survey systems. **AutoEvalMetrics** [arXiv:2503.05712] investigates whether citation count and review score prediction can serve as automatic evaluation signals, finding moderate correlations with human quality ratings (r ≈ 0.4–0.5).

Traditional NLP metrics such as ROUGE and BLEU remain in use for survey evaluation, but multiple studies have established their limitations for this task: they measure lexical overlap rather than content accuracy, fail to penalize hallucinated citations, and correlate weakly with human judgements for long-form text [arXiv:2401.15042].

### 6.4 Hallucination and Factuality Evaluation

Hallucination detection for survey text draws on a broader literature of factuality evaluation for LLMs. Beyond the survey-specific methods discussed in Section 5.4, several general-purpose hallucination benchmarks and detectors are applicable to the survey domain. These methods can be grouped by their underlying approach.

**Benchmark-based methods** provide standardized corpora for evaluating hallucination detectors. **HaluEval** [arXiv:2305.11747] provides a large-scale hallucination evaluation benchmark with approximately 19.5% hallucination rate across tasks, offering a standardized corpus for testing detection methods. **HALoGEN** [arXiv:2501.08292] covers 10,923 prompts across 9 domains with a fine-grained error classification (Type A: incorrect recollection — the model recalls a fact incorrectly; Type B: incorrect knowledge — the model has no relevant knowledge; Type C: fabrication — the model invents a fact), providing a taxonomy that maps directly to survey generation failure modes.

**Sampling-based methods** require no external database. **SelfCheckGPT** [arXiv:2303.08896] generates multiple samples from the LLM and checks for consistency between them — if a claim appears in only one of several samples, it is flagged as a potential hallucination. This approach is particularly applicable to survey text where the external evidence base may be incomplete.

**Meta-evaluation methods** assess the reliability of factuality metrics themselves. **TRUE** [arXiv:2204.04991] provides a meta-evaluation of 11 factuality consistency datasets, establishing evaluation protocols and identifying systematic biases in existing metrics.

**NLI-based methods** operate as lightweight verification layers. **Provenance** [arXiv:2411.01022] offers an NLI-based factuality checker for RAG output that can be deployed as a post-generation verification step in survey pipelines.

**Domain-specific methods** address the varying hallucination patterns across scientific fields. **DAHL** [arXiv:2411.09255] focuses on domain-specific hallucination evaluation for biomedicine, identifying that hallucination patterns in specialized domains differ from general-domain text and require domain-specific evaluation protocols.

For survey generation, the most effective hallucination detection approach combines multiple signals: checking citations against a verified paper database (to detect fabricated references), verifying claims against source texts using NLI models (to detect misattribution), and sampling-based consistency checks (to detect unsupported claims). No single method is sufficient, and the field lacks a dedicated hallucination benchmark for survey text specifically [arXiv:2305.14251; arXiv:2406.19276].

### 6.5 Human Evaluation Protocols

Despite advances in automatic evaluation, human evaluation remains the gold standard for survey quality assessment. The dimensions typically evaluated include: **coverage** (does the survey adequately cover the topic's sub-areas?), **coherence** (do sections flow logically and connect to each other?), **accuracy** (are claims correctly attributed and factually sound?), **readability** (is the prose clear and well-organized?), and **usefulness** (would the survey help a researcher new to the field?). The **QUAL-SG framework** from SurveyGen [arXiv:2508.17647] provides a structured protocol for human evaluation of survey generation, specifying annotation guidelines, rating scales, and inter-annotator agreement targets (Cohen's κ > 0.6). SurveyBench [arXiv:2510.03120] and SurGE [arXiv:2508.15658] include human evaluation components with 3–5 annotators per survey and report moderate-to-high inter-annotator agreement (Cohen's κ = 0.55–0.75 across dimensions). SurveyEval [arXiv:2512.02763] combines LLM-as-a-Judge with human evaluation, finding that LLM judges correlate with human ratings at r ≈ 0.5–0.6 — moderate correlation that suggests LLM evaluation can complement but not replace human assessment.

Key challenges in human evaluation include: annotation cost (a thorough evaluation of a single survey takes 20–40 minutes), inter-annotator variability (different annotators weight dimensions differently), and the need for domain-expert annotators (evaluating survey accuracy requires knowledge of the field). These challenges motivate the development of better automatic metrics while acknowledging that human evaluation will remain essential for reliable quality assessment [arXiv:2508.17647; arXiv:2510.03120].

## 7. Emerging Frontiers: Interactive, Living, and Coordinated Surveys

### Personalized generation, continuous updating, and advanced multi-agent coordination

The systems described in Sections 2–5 largely follow a static, one-shot generation paradigm: a topic is specified, papers are retrieved, and a survey is produced as a final artifact. A growing body of work pushes beyond this paradigm toward interactive and personalized generation, living surveys that update over time, and more sophisticated coordination patterns for multi-agent systems. This section surveys these emerging directions, which collectively blur the boundary between survey generation and scientific discovery.

### 7.1 Interactive and Personalized Survey Generation

Rather than treating survey generation as a fully automatic process, interactive systems involve the user at key decision points, enabling personalization and expert guidance. **InteractiveSurvey** (described in Section 2.3) allows users to customize the outline, the paper pool, and section-level drafting parameters through a GUI-based interaction loop, with the system regenerating affected sections based on user input. **ChatCite** [arXiv:2403.02574] takes a conversational approach, guiding users through the survey creation process with a structured workflow that combines LLM generation with human decision-making at each stage — topic refinement, paper selection, outline construction, and text revision. **DimInd** [arXiv:2504.18496] introduces facet-based synthesis where users specify analytical facets (e.g., "compare methods by accuracy vs. efficiency") and the system retrieves and synthesizes evidence for each facet, enabling personalized comparative surveys. **Synergi** [arXiv:2308.07517] develops a mixed-initiative scholarly synthesis system where the LLM proposes content and the human refines it, with the system learning from user edits to improve future proposals.

The spectrum from fully automatic to fully manual is continuous. At one extreme are systems like Agentic AutoSurvey [arXiv:2509.18661] with no user involvement; at the other are collaborative writing environments where the LLM acts as an intelligent assistant rather than an autonomous author. Interactive systems occupy the productive middle, addressing the limitation that one-shot automatic surveys cannot capture domain-specific expertise or personal preferences [arXiv:2403.02574]. A key open question is determining the optimal interaction frequency and grain — how many user decisions yield the best quality-cost trade-off.

### 7.2 Living and Continuously Updated Surveys

A static survey is outdated as soon as new relevant papers are published. Living survey systems address this by supporting continuous or periodic updates. **vitaLITy 2** [arXiv:2408.13450] implements a RAG architecture for iterative literature search and summarization, where the system maintains a persistent paper database and regenerates survey sections when new papers are added. It supports scheduled updates (e.g., weekly re-generation) and can alert users when papers in their domain trigger significant changes to survey content. **Evolving Literature Analysis** [arXiv:2502.18791] develops a semi-automated pipeline for longitudinal tracking of literature evolution, combining automated retrieval with periodic human validation to maintain quality across updates. **Completing Systematic Reviews in Hours** [arXiv:2504.14822] introduces InsightAgent, a human-centered interactive workflow for medical systematic reviews that can produce evidence syntheses in hours rather than months, with built-in support for updating the review as new trials are published.

Living survey systems face unique challenges: maintaining citation consistency across updates (new claims must integrate with existing text without contradictions), managing version control for survey content, and deciding when updates are warranted (every new paper, or only significant contributions). The broader paradigm of living reviews, established in medical literature [arXiv:1909.06758; arXiv:2004.06183], provides methodological guidance but has not been fully operationalized for LLM-based systems [arXiv:2408.13450; arXiv:2502.18791].

### 7.3 Advanced Multi-Agent Coordination Patterns

While current multi-agent survey systems use relatively simple coordination protocols (sequential pipeline or roundtable discussion, Section 2.2), the broader multi-agent coordination literature offers more sophisticated patterns that could improve survey generation. **AgensFlow** [arXiv:2605.27466] introduces a learned routing mechanism for multi-agent coordination, where a coordination policy is trained to route sub-tasks to the most capable agent based on task characteristics — potentially enabling dynamic agent composition for different survey sections. **KABB** (Knowledge-Aware Bayesian Bandits) [arXiv:2502.07350] provides a mechanism for dynamic expert selection, selecting which specialized agent to consult for each claim based on semantic understanding of the claim and historical agent accuracy. **Federation of Agents** [arXiv:2509.20175] proposes a semantics-aware communication fabric where agents share structured representations (not raw text), reducing token costs and improving information flow quality. **AgentCoord** [arXiv:2404.11943] offers a visual exploration tool for designing coordination strategies, enabling system builders to prototype and analyze survey-specific coordination patterns before deployment. The multi-agent collaborative workflow in **LiRA** [arXiv:2510.05138], discussed in Section 2.2, exemplifies the roundtable discussion pattern that these more advanced coordination methods could further optimize.

These coordination patterns have not yet been applied to survey generation, but they address known limitations: static agent role assignment, high token costs from unstructured inter-agent communication, and the inability to dynamically compose agent teams for different survey tasks. Their application to survey generation represents a promising research direction [arXiv:2605.27466; arXiv:2502.07350].

### 7.4 OpenScholar and Scientific Synthesis Systems

**OpenScholar** [arXiv:2411.14199] represents a significant step beyond traditional survey generation toward comprehensive scientific synthesis. Rather than generating a survey from a set of retrieved papers, OpenScholar maintains a 45-million-paper datastore and supports interactive scientific reasoning — answering questions, comparing methods, and generating synthesis text — grounded in the full open-access literature. **ResearchAgent** [arXiv:2404.07738] extends the survey paradigm toward research idea generation, using a multi-agent architecture to survey literature, identify gaps, and propose novel research directions based on synthesis of existing work. **ResearchPilot** [arXiv:2603.14629] combines local-first multi-agent literature synthesis with DSPy-driven optimization, enabling researchers to deploy survey generation on their own infrastructure without API dependencies.

These systems illustrate how surveys are not just summaries of what is known but structured analyses that can identify gaps, synthesize competing claims, and suggest future work. Notably, the coordination patterns discussed in Section 7.3 — learned routing (AgensFlow), dynamic expert selection (KABB), semantics-aware communication (Federation of Agents), and coordination strategy design (AgentCoord) — have direct relevance to OpenScholar's modular retriever-reader pipeline and ResearchPilot's multi-agent workflow. Applying these patterns could enable more dynamic agent composition in scientific synthesis systems, where specialized agents are selected and routed based on the type of synthesis task (e.g., method comparison vs. gap identification) rather than relying on static role assignment. As LLM-based synthesis capabilities improve, the distinction between writing a survey and conducting a literature analysis is likely to dissolve entirely [arXiv:2411.14199; arXiv:2404.07738].

## 8. Open Challenges and Future Directions

### Key limitations, research gaps, and promising avenues in automated survey generation

Despite significant progress across the dimensions reviewed in Sections 2–7, several fundamental challenges remain unresolved. This section synthesizes the major open challenges, identifies research gaps, and proposes promising future directions, organized around six themes.

### 8.1 Hallucination and Citation Fabrication

Hallucination in survey generation manifests differently than in QA or summarization. Rather than generating plausible-sounding but incorrect facts about a known entity, survey generation hallucination typically takes three forms: **fabricated citations** (references to non-existent papers or incorrect venues), **misattributed claims** (claiming a paper said something it did not), and **outdated facts** (citing a paper that has been superseded or retracted). SciReviewGen's analysis [arXiv:2305.15186] found that LLM-generated surveys exhibit hallucination rates of 15–25% for factual claims, with citation fabrication being the most common form. The FActScore decomposition method [arXiv:2305.14251] (discussed in Section 5.4) can be applied to survey text, but verifying hundreds of atomic facts per survey remains labor-intensive without automation. SAFE's search-augmented evaluation [arXiv:2403.18802] offers a more scalable alternative, though its reliance on web search may not capture the full evidence base available in restricted-access scientific databases. SelfCheckGPT's sampling-based detection [arXiv:2303.08896] requires no external databases but has not been validated on scientific survey text specifically.

Addressing survey-specific hallucination requires an integrated pipeline combining three components: (a) improved retrieval pipelines that provide sufficient evidence for each claim (Section 4), (b) fine-grained citation mechanisms that ground each claim to a specific source passage (Section 5.1), and (c) post-generation verification against a trusted paper database (Section 5.4). The integration of these three components into a unified mitigation pipeline — where verification failures feed back into improved retrieval and citation — remains an open research problem that no current system fully addresses.

### 8.2 Evaluation Standardization

The proliferation of evaluation benchmarks and metrics (Section 6) has created a fragmented landscape where cross-system comparison is difficult. SurveyBench (described in Section 6.1), SurGE [arXiv:2508.15658], SurveyEval [arXiv:2512.02763], and SurveyLens [arXiv:2602.11238] each use different evaluation dimensions, different scoring methodologies, and different topic sets. A system that scores highly on one benchmark may perform poorly on another, and the lack of a shared evaluation framework makes it difficult to assess genuine progress. Among these benchmarks, the concept of **coverage breadth** — measuring how comprehensively a survey covers the relevant sub-areas of a topic — appears under different names and operationalizations, from SurveyBench's topic-overlap metric to SurGE's coverage dimension to SurveyLens's discipline-specific rubrics. Standardizing this and other evaluation dimensions would substantially improve cross-system comparability.

LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation, while scalable, introduces systematic biases — LLMs tend to favor longer, more fluent outputs over concise, accurate ones, and may prefer writing styles that mirror the LLM's own training distribution [arXiv:2510.03120]. The LitLLMs evaluation study [arXiv:2412.15249] raises the concern of data contamination: benchmark topics may overlap with LLM training data, inflating reported performance. A unified evaluation framework — with standardized dimensions, scoring protocols, and contamination-controlled topic sets — would substantially accelerate progress in the field. The GLUE (General Language Understanding Evaluation) benchmark in general NLP — with its standardized training, leaderboard, and scoring protocol — offers a precedent for how such standardization can catalyze a research community. Until such a framework emerges, practitioners should evaluate their systems on multiple benchmarks and report all dimensions separately, rather than relying on a single aggregate score.

### 8.3 Knowledge Freshness and Temporal Dynamics

Surveys become stale from the moment of publication, and most current systems have no mechanism for updating content as new papers appear. The living survey systems discussed in Section 7.2 — vitaLITy 2 [arXiv:2408.13450] and Evolving Literature Analysis [arXiv:2502.18791] — represent initial attempts, but they face significant challenges. Maintaining narrative coherence across updates is difficult because new evidence may contradict or refine earlier claims, requiring the system to revise not just individual sentences but the logical structure of affected sections. Version management for cited content — distinguishing between different versions of the same paper, tracking retractions, and managing preprint-to-published transitions — is an under-explored problem with no standardized solution. Determining update triggers is equally challenging: a policy of updating for every new paper generates noise, while a policy of updating only for "significant" contributions requires a definition of significance that is domain-dependent and difficult to operationalize.

A concrete example illustrates the challenge: a system might cite a 2025 result as "the current state of the art" without recognizing that a 2024 survey already covered the 2020–2023 literature on the same topic, or that a 2026 paper has since improved upon the 2025 result. Temporal reasoning — distinguishing whether a claim is still valid, has been superseded, or has been refined — is a capability that current systems lack. Existing temporal reasoning benchmarks from the NLP literature could inform the development of such capabilities, but no survey generation system currently incorporates temporal awareness into its retrieval or citation pipeline. This gap is particularly acute for rapidly evolving fields such as AI, where the half-life of a factual claim can be measured in months rather than years, and where a survey that fails to account for the latest developments may actively mislead its readers.

### 8.4 Domain Adaptation and Cost

Most survey generation systems have been developed and evaluated on computer science and biomedical literature — domains with well-structured paper databases (PubMed, arXiv, ACL Anthology), established citation practices, and active NLP communities. Adapting these systems to new domains (e.g., social sciences, humanities, engineering) requires addressing differences in discourse structure, citation conventions, and paper database availability. Social science surveys, for example, may reference books, government reports, and grey literature alongside peer-reviewed articles, requiring retrieval pipelines that can handle heterogeneous source types. Humanities surveys often employ narrative structures that differ from the "related work → method → results" pattern common in CS surveys, challenging the outline heuristics learned from computer science corpora. Domain-specific terminology presents challenges for both retrieval (queries may use different vocabularies than the target literature) and evaluation (domain experts are needed for human evaluation).

The cost of generating comprehensive surveys further limits accessibility. Token costs for multi-agent systems can exceed $10–$50 per survey for API-based LLMs, making systematic experimentation and iterative refinement expensive. ResearchPilot [arXiv:2603.14629] and similar local-first initiatives address cost by using smaller, locally deployable models, but raise questions about quality — the trade-off between model scale and output quality is particularly acute for multi-agent systems where each agent's reasoning quality contributes to the final output. Developing cost-effective survey generation that maintains high quality across diverse domains remains an open engineering and research challenge.

### 8.5 Multi-Modal and Non-Textual Content

Current survey generation systems operate almost exclusively on text, yet scientific communication relies heavily on figures, tables, equations, and code. A survey that describes a method without showing its results table, or discusses a model architecture without reproducing its diagram, provides an incomplete synthesis. The specific types of non-textual content that current systems miss include: system architecture diagrams (common in ML papers), mathematical derivations and proofs (essential in theoretical work), empirical result tables with performance comparisons (critical for method evaluation), algorithm pseudocode (needed for reproducibility), and data visualization plots (scatter plots, loss curves, confusion matrices). None of these content types are generated or synthesized by current survey systems, which limits the practical utility of automated surveys for researchers who need to compare methods at a glance.

Multi-modal LLMs — such as GPT-4V, which can interpret images and diagrams, and LLaVA, which extends visual instruction tuning to large language models — offer a potential foundation for non-textual survey content. These models could, in principle, extract information from source-paper figures and tables, generate textual descriptions of visual content, and even produce summary tables that aggregate quantitative results across cited papers. However, the integration of multi-modal capabilities into survey generation pipelines raises challenges: output formats must be structured (tables require defined schemas, diagrams require layout specifications), hallucinations in visual interpretation can compound errors in downstream text, and the token cost of processing images in context is substantially higher than text-only processing. No current system has demonstrated multi-modal survey generation, making this one of the most important open challenges in the field.

### 8.6 User Steering and Controllability

The tension between full automation and user control remains unresolved. Fully automatic systems (Sections 2.1–2.2) offer efficiency — a survey can be generated in minutes with no user effort — but may produce surveys that miss domain-specific perspectives or fail to reflect personal preferences about emphasis, depth, and framing. Interactive systems (Section 7.1) provide fine-grained control but require significant user effort, potentially consuming as much time as writing the survey manually. Finding the optimal balance — where users provide minimal guidance that maximally improves survey quality — requires understanding the relationship between user input characteristics and output quality improvements.

Current interactive systems offer different interaction patterns. ChatCite [arXiv:2403.02574] uses a conversational workflow where the user makes sequential decisions at each stage of survey creation, providing high control but requiring sustained attention. InteractiveSurvey (described in Section 2.3) offers a GUI-based approach where users can intervene at specific stages (outline, paper pool, section parameters) while the system handles the rest autonomously, providing more flexible control. Synergi [arXiv:2308.07517] adopts a mixed-initiative model where the system proposes content and the user refines it, with the system learning from edits over time, reducing the user's per-session effort. Systematic studies are needed to compare these interaction patterns along dimensions of user effort, output quality, and user satisfaction. Key design questions include: at which stage of the pipeline does user input have the greatest marginal impact? How should systems present trade-offs between competing objectives (e.g., coverage vs. depth) to enable informed user decisions? Can systems learn user preferences across sessions to reduce the guidance required for each new survey? Addressing these questions is essential for designing the next generation of steerable survey systems that achieve a favorable point on the automation-control frontier.

## 9. Conclusion

### Synthesis, practical recommendations, and a research agenda

Automated literature survey generation using large language models has matured from a proof-of-concept into a vibrant research area with dozens of systems, benchmarks, and evaluation protocols. This survey has organized the landscape along three architectural axes — single-agent, multi-agent, and hybrid — and examined how these architectures address planning, retrieval, citation, and evaluation.

**Architectural recommendations.** For practitioners building a survey generation system, the choice of architecture depends on available resources and requirements. Single-agent systems (AutoSurvey, SurveyX, SurveyForge) are appropriate for constrained domains with well-defined paper corpora, offering simplicity and low deployment cost. Multi-agent systems (Agentic AutoSurvey, SurveyG, LiRA) are recommended for broad-coverage surveys where quality is paramount, yielding 15–25% higher benchmark scores at the cost of increased complexity and token consumption. Hybrid systems (AutoSurvey2, InteractiveSurvey) are best suited for expert-guided survey projects where domain-specific knowledge must be incorporated, or high-stakes applications where human verification is mandatory.

**Evaluation checklist.** New survey generation systems should be evaluated along at least three dimensions: citation precision (the fraction of citations that correctly support their associated claims), factual consistency (the fraction of claims that are supported by cited sources), and coverage breadth (the fraction of known relevant topics covered). For general-domain evaluation, SurveyBench provides the most comprehensive benchmark; for computer science specifically, SurGE offers a larger retrieval pool and domain-specific dimensions. Systems should be evaluated on contamination-controlled topic sets (following the LitLLMs evaluation study protocol) and reported with per-dimension scores rather than a single aggregate.

**Research agenda.** The three most impactful directions for future work are: (1) **evaluation standardization** — developing a unified evaluation framework with shared dimensions, contamination-controlled topics, and standardized scoring protocols, modeled on precedents like GLUE (General Language Understanding Evaluation) in general NLP; (2) **citation factuality and verification** — moving beyond citation existence checks to automatic verification that generated claims accurately represent cited papers' content, combining fine-grained attribution (sub-sentence level), post-generation verification (NLI-based), and fine-tuning for hallucination resistance; and (3) **interactive and living surveys** — building systems that support user steering, personalization, and continuous updating, addressing the fundamental limitation that static one-shot surveys cannot capture evolving literature or domain-specific expertise.

As LLM capabilities continue to advance, the boundary between survey generation and scientific discovery will blur. Systems that can synthesize literature, identify gaps, and propose new research directions will transform how researchers engage with the scientific literature. Realizing this vision requires not just better models but better architectures — systems that retrieve with completeness, cite with precision, evaluate with rigor, and interact with insight. The foundations laid by the work surveyed in this paper provide a strong starting point for that journey.
